Notes from the field.

Short, practical notes on capability building, learning ROI, talent and HR transformation.

A workshop can build awareness, but it rarely changes results on its own. What decides whether learning sticks is everything around it: clear expectations from the manager, time to practise, tools that support the new way of working, and a measure people care about. Before designing any programme, I ask what should be different in the business in three months, and what else besides training must change to make that happen. Often the answer includes a job aid, a process fix or a better conversation between a manager and their team.

First, start from a business problem, not a course catalogue. Second, win one senior sponsor early. Third, pilot small and fix quickly. Fourth, make managers your partners, because they decide whether learning is used. Fifth, measure something simple and report it every quarter. In my experience a learning function earns trust when people see a result they care about, not when the training calendar gets longer.

Start with a baseline before the programme begins and pick one business measure, such as errors, time to competence or sales per person. Estimate benefits conservatively and count only what training itself caused. Compare them with the full cost, including people’s time. ROI is net benefit divided by cost. If money is hard to measure, track behaviour change after 30 to 60 days and ask managers what they see. You can try the calculator on this page with your own numbers.

AI will change tasks faster than it changes job titles. Build three things: AI literacy for everyone, including safe use and checking output; stronger human skills such as judgement, communication and learning agility; and learning that happens in the flow of work, with short modules and job aids. Map which tasks in each role are likely to change, then plan reskilling and internal mobility around those tasks. Start with one team and learn from it.

Filling a vacancy solves today’s problem. A pipeline solves next year’s. Define what success looks like in each critical role, use structured interviews so every candidate is assessed on the same things, and design onboarding as a 30-60-90 day journey. Then connect hiring to development through mentoring, stretch assignments and succession plans. The best test of a recruitment process is how well people perform and grow six months after they join.

Pick a small, real problem, such as helping new joiners find answers faster. Talk to five people who face it. Build the simplest thing that could help, like a one-page guide or a five-minute video. Test it with a few users, improve it, then measure what changed. Finish with a one-page story: the problem, what you built and the result.
